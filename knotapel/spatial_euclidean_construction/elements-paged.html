Definitions

Definition I.1 — Point
A point is an ordered triple of real numbers (x, y, z) specifying a position in three-dimensional Euclidean space.
Definition I.2 — Line
A line is the unique straight path extending infinitely in both directions through two distinct points P and Q. We write it as L(P, Q). Parametrically:
L(t) = P + t(Q − P), t ∈ ℝ
Definition I.3 — Plane
A plane is the unique flat surface containing three non-collinear points P, Q, R. We write it as Π(P, Q, R). Its normal vector is:
n = (Q − P) × (R − P)
and the plane equation is:
n · (X − P) = 0
Definition I.4 — Sphere
A sphere is the set of all points equidistant from a center point C, where the radius equals the distance from C to a given point P. We write it as S(C, P).
|X − C|² = |P − C|²
Points, lines, planes, and spheres. Points are the atomic objects (ordered triples). Lines, planes, and spheres are constructed from points via the three postulates. Circles also exist but only as theoretical intermediates (Definition I.5). It turns a geometric object (a line) into an algebraic one: plug in a value of t, get a point. This makes intersections computable — substitute into another equation and solve for t. The rational theory T_Q depends on this: line-plane intersection is just solving a linear equation in t. In 2D, the compass draws a circle (a 1D curve). In 3D, S(C,P) gives a sphere (a 2D surface). This is why sphere-sphere intersection yields a circle (1D) and not discrete points — you need a third object to cut down to 0D (points). The whole intersection catalog follows from this dimension arithmetic.
Definition I.5 — Circle in Space
A circle in three-dimensional space is the intersection of a sphere and a plane. It is characterized by a center point, a radius, and the plane in which it lies. A circle is not constructible by our postulates; it exists only as the intersection of a sphere and a plane, and is never an input to further constructions — only a theoretical intermediate.
Definition I.6 — General Position
Points are in general position if no three are collinear and no four are coplanar. Equivalently, every subset of the points spans the maximum possible dimension.
Definition I.7 — Power of a Point
The power of a point X with respect to a sphere S(C, P) with radius r is the quantity:
pow(X, S) = |X − C|² − r²
Points on the sphere have power 0. Points outside the sphere have positive power; points inside have negative power.
Definition I.8 — Constructible Point
A point is constructible from an initial set of points if it can be obtained by a finite sequence of:
  1. Constructing lines, planes, or spheres from known points
  2. Intersecting constructed objects to obtain new points
A point is rationally constructible if it can be obtained using only lines and planes (no spheres).
Our three postulates take only points as inputs: L(P,Q) needs two points, Π(P,Q,R) needs three, S(C,P) needs two. A circle is a continuous curve, not a point, so it can't feed into any postulate. We can only extract points from circles by intersecting with another object. Power is the algebraic quantity that governs when spheres cooperate to produce intersection points. The radical plane (Book III) is exactly the locus where two spheres have equal power. This drives the Bootstrap — the first moment where new points appear. Definition I.8 sets up the T_Q / T_E split that Definition I.10 formalizes. Rationally constructible = T_Q (lines + planes). Constructible = T_E (lines + planes + spheres). The difference is whether square roots can appear.

Postulates

Postulate I — The Line
Given any two distinct points P and Q, we can construct the line L(P, Q) through them.
Postulate II — The Plane
Given any three non-collinear points P, Q, R, we can construct the plane Π(P, Q, R) containing them.
Postulate III — The Sphere
Given any two distinct points C and P, we can construct the sphere S(C, P) centered at C passing through P.
Note
These three postulates are the 3D analogues of Euclid's first and third postulates. The straightedge gives us Postulates I and II; the compass gives us Postulate III. Postulate II has no 2D analogue — it is genuinely new in three dimensions.
Postulate II (the Plane). In 2D, two points already determine the unique line in the ambient space. In 3D, three points determine a plane, which is a genuinely new kind of object with no 2D counterpart. This gives T_Q, the rational theory. Theorem II.4 (Tetrahedron Inertness) shows the initial four points produce nothing new under lines and planes alone. The theory is closed and trivial. Spheres are what break inertness. Euclid's 1st: draw a line between two points → our Postulate I. Euclid's 3rd: draw a circle with center and radius → our Postulate III (sphere replaces circle). Euclid's 2nd (extend a line) and 4th/5th (right angles, parallel postulate) have no direct analogues here.

The Initial Configuration

Every construction must begin somewhere. We require an initial set of points that is non-degenerate — in 3D, this means at least four points in general position (Definition I.6), so that we can span all three dimensions.

Definition I.9 — The Standard Initial Configuration
Our standard starting points are:
O = (0, 0, 0)  A = (1, 0, 0)  B = (0, 1, 0)  C = (0, 0, 1)
These four points are in general position and all have rational coordinates. They form a tetrahedron with one vertex at the origin and the other three at unit distance along each axis.
Note
The choice of initial configuration affects what is constructible. The standard configuration establishes the unit length and the three coordinate axes. Any other set of four rational points in general position would yield an equivalent theory (up to affine transformation), but this choice is convenient for computation.
Convenience. Any four rational points in general position give an equivalent theory (up to affine transformation). But O=(0,0,0), A=(1,0,0), B=(0,1,0), C=(0,0,1) establish the unit length and align with the coordinate system, making formulas cleaner. This is one of the central recurring objects of the treatise. It accumulates 6+ equivalent characterizations across the books: as the face opposite the origin, as a radical plane, as a barycentric reference plane, and more. It is the most natural non-coordinate plane in the setup. Yes, and we'd have more raw material. But 4 is the minimal non-degenerate set in 3D. More points don't change the theory — even with exactly 4, T_E is rich enough to construct everything in the constructible field.

A plane slicing through a sphere gives a circle (a continuous curve), not discrete points. Our postulates take points as inputs. To extract points from a circle, intersect it with another object — which is why three-object intersections appear. The resulting coordinates may involve square roots of rationals. The Two Theories

Definition I.10 — The Two Theories

TQ (The Rational Theory): Only Postulates I and II are used. All intersections remain in ℚ. The constructible field is ℚ itself.

TE (The Euclidean Theory): All three postulates are used. The constructible field is a tower of quadratic extensions over ℚ.

Theorem (Preview) — Tetrahedron Inertness
In TQ, the initial four points produce only themselves — no new points are constructible using lines and planes alone. The tetrahedron is inert under TQ.

Proof: See Book II, Theorem 4.

Theorem (Preview) — The Bootstrap
In TE, the initial four points plus spheres produce 12 new points in the first generation, for a total of 16. This breaks the inertness of TQ.

Proof: See Book III, Proposition 4.

T_Q uses only lines and planes and produces no new points from the initial tetrahedron; T_E adds spheres and can construct any point whose coordinates lie in a tower of quadratic extensions over ℚ. Each pair of original points gives two spheres: S(P,Q) and S(Q,P). Triples of spheres can intersect in discrete points. The exact count of 12 comes from the geometry of the initial configuration. Full proof is in Book III, Proposition 4. In 2D, two points + circles immediately produce new points. In 3D, lines and planes alone produce nothing new (inertness). The sphere is needed to break the higher-dimensional lock. This is the central surprise of the 3D theory.

Proposition 1: Line ∩ Plane

Proposition II.1 — Line ∩ Plane

Given a line L(P, Q) and a plane Π with normal n passing through point R, the intersection point (if it exists) is:

X = P + t(Q − P), where t = n · (R − P) / n · (Q − P)

The intersection exists iff n · (Q − P) ≠ 0 (i.e., the line is not parallel to the plane).

Proof. Substitute L(t) into the plane equation n · (X − R) = 0 and solve for t. The result is a rational function of the input coordinates, so the intersection point has rational coordinates whenever the inputs do.

▮
The line is parametric (linear in t) and the plane equation is linear. Substituting gives a linear equation in t, which solves to t = (rational expression). No square roots appear because we never solve a quadratic. The line direction Q-P is perpendicular to the plane normal n, so the line is parallel to the plane. If the line also lies in the plane, every point is an intersection. Otherwise there is none.

Proposition 2: Plane ∩ Plane

Proposition II.2 — Plane ∩ Plane

Two non-parallel planes Π1 and Π2 intersect in a line. The direction of this line is:

d = n1 × n2

A point on the line is found by solving a 2×3 linear system (the two plane equations). All coordinates remain rational.

▮
The intersection line must lie in both planes, so it must be perpendicular to both normals. The cross product gives precisely the vector perpendicular to both. If normals are parallel, the cross product is zero.

Proposition 3: Line ∩ Line

Proposition II.3 — Line ∩ Line

Two coplanar, non-parallel lines L(P1, Q1) and L(P2, Q2) intersect in exactly one point, found by solving:

P1 + s(Q1 − P1) = P2 + t(Q2 − P2)

In 3D, two generic lines are skew (non-intersecting, non-parallel). Intersection occurs iff the lines are coplanar, which can be tested by the scalar triple product condition.

▮
In 2D, two lines either intersect or are parallel — they share the same plane (the entire 2D space). In 3D, lines can lie in different planes, failing to intersect without being parallel. The scalar triple product detects coplanarity.

Theorem 4: Tetrahedron Inertness

Theorem II.4 — Tetrahedron Inertness

The four initial points O, A, B, C in general position produce no new points under TQ (lines and planes only).

Proof. From four points we can construct:

  • Lines: C(4,2) = 6 lines
  • Planes: C(4,3) = 4 planes

We check all possible intersections:

  • Line ∩ Plane: Each line either lies in a plane (giving no new point) or intersects it at one of the original four points.
  • Plane ∩ Plane: Each pair of planes intersects in a line already in our set (a line through two of the original points).
  • Line ∩ Line: Each pair of coplanar lines intersects at one of the original four points.

Every intersection reproduces an existing point. The set {O, A, B, C} is closed under TQ-construction.

▮
Scholium — Why Inertness Matters
This result is shocking from the perspective of classical 2D geometry, where two points immediately generate a rich structure. In 3D with rational constructions alone, four points produce nothing new. This is the fundamental obstruction that motivates TE and the use of spheres.
Lines: choose 2 from 4 points = C(4,2) = 6. Planes: choose 3 from 4 = C(4,3) = 4. These are ALL distinct lines and planes constructible from just 4 points. In 2D, start with 2 points. Draw circles centered at each through the other. They intersect in 2 new points. The constructible set grows rapidly. In 3D with lines and planes only, the growth is zero. The geometry is locked. Yes. The proof is purely combinatorial — it counts lines and planes and checks all intersections. The specific coordinates don't matter; only the general position hypothesis (no degeneracies) is used.
