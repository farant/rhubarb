Knotapel

Knots as computation: demos 1 to 50 as built, and a new demo 113

Knotapel is a research project in knotapel/: a little over a hundred numbered demos, each a single C89 file with its own tests, no dependencies and, as far as possible, no floating point. Its main line is Discrete Knotted Computation (DKC): evaluated at a particular root of unity, the Kauffman bracket of a knot is an exact cyclotomic integer, and those values turn out to work as the weights of a neural network that computes Boolean functions, with no training (demo 29 showed it for XOR; later demos map out all thirteen classes of three-input functions, and where parity hits a wall). The project keeps its own atlas (knotapel/atlas/, with a web version) and a long explorer's log; it was worked by a small team of Claude sessions with Fran, and it keeps its English rather than the house's Latin.

The first ten demos are the foundation under all of that. Demos 1 to 5 compute the same thing, the Kauffman bracket, in a new way each time and check each new way against the old one; by demo 5 the bracket of a braid on any number of strands is the trace of a product of matrices, computed exactly. Demos 6 to 10 explain the formula's weights, connect the two ways of writing a knot in both directions, prove the bracket behaves correctly under the moves that don't change a knot, and finally trade exact polynomials for numbers. Demos 11 to 15 use those numbers for a first attempt at "compiling" knots into weights: the bracket as a signal over the circle, how few bits identify a knot, and which angles to sample. Demos 16 to 20 refine that signal and then turn it to logic: braids whose bracket, thresholded, acts as a NOT or a NAND gate, a small compiler of Boolean circuits from those gates, and a map of where the gates stop working. Demo 19 is where A = ei5π/4, the root of unity at which δ = 0, first takes centre stage. Demos 21 to 25 survey what braids at δ = 0 can compute: reversible gates, the shortest braid for every Boolean function, the zoo of functions topology prefers, the arithmetic of rational tangles, and the four closures of a braid read as four measurements. Three-input parity first shows up here, as the function no braid produces. Demos 26 to 29 are the turn: bracket values as the weights of a neuron, first by matching trained weights to them (26 to 28), then by using them directly with no training at all (29). Numbers 30 to 34 were planned but never built as code (their plans are in knotapel/future-demos/); demo 35, the next one built, lays the exact Temperley–Lieb machinery for everything after. The numbering is not chronological from here: demo 38 says its code comes "from Demo 60", so later work filled earlier planned numbers. Demos 38 and 39 study the algebra's structure at δ = 0; demo 45 opens the arc the atlas calls landmark, taking reverse DKC from XOR to all thirteen classes of three-input functions, and demos 46 to 50 follow it to its headline: three-input parity, unreachable for every bracket weight under one activation, reachable under another with six sectors.

Where reading the code turned up a mistake or a weak check, it is described in Notes from this reading at the end, and marked in the demo's section with a pointer.

Words you need

Kauffman bracket ⟨K⟩a polynomial computed from a knot diagram by "smoothing" each crossing one of two ways (an A-smoothing or a B-smoothing), counting the loops that result, and summing over every choice. Each smoothing contributes A or A−1; each loop beyond the first contributes δ = −A2 − A−2.
Laurent polynomiala polynomial in A that may have negative powers, here with integer coefficients, so all arithmetic is exact.
writhe w, f-polynomialthe bracket changes when a diagram gains a kink; multiplying by (−A3)∓w (w = the sum of crossing signs) removes that, giving the Jones polynomial up to a change of variable.
braid wordstrands side by side; σi crosses strand i over i+1, σi−1 under. Joining the tops to the bottoms (the closure) gives a knot or link, and every knot arises this way.
Temperley–Lieb Uithe "cup-cap": strands i and i+1 turned back on themselves. Ui2 = δ·Ui, because doing it twice leaves a closed loop.

Demo 01 · The bracket by brute force

knotapel/demo_01_kauffman_bracket/main.c · 611 lines · 9/9 tests

The question was the smallest one the project could ask: can exact knot invariants be computed from a diagram with nothing but integer arithmetic? The demo builds a Laurent polynomial type (an array of integer coefficients plus the lowest exponent), reads knots in PD notation (each crossing lists the four arcs that meet there, counter-clockwise), and computes the bracket as a state sum: for each of the 2n ways to smooth n crossings it counts the resulting loops by walking from arc to partner arc around each cycle, then adds A#A−#B·δloops−1.

It reproduces the known brackets of the trefoil, the figure-eight (palindromic, as it must be for a knot that equals its mirror image) and the Hopf link, and it tells the trefoil from the figure-eight. Two lessons are written down in its README: the counter-clockwise PD convention silently decides chirality (the "right-handed" trefoil came out left-handed, which is the same bracket with A and A−1 exchanged), and loop counting was the hard part; the first attempt was wrong. The result is about 500 lines of C89, exponential in the number of crossings, and from here on it is the oracle that every faster method is checked against. (Its bracket values are right; the f-polynomial it prints for the trefoil is not, see note 2.)

⟨trefoil⟩     = −A^-5 − A^3 + A^7
⟨figure-8⟩    = A^-8 − A^-4 + 1 − A^4 + A^8
⟨Hopf link⟩   = −A^-4 − A^4

Demo 02 · From diagrams to braid words

knotapel/demo_02_braid_words/main.c · 1,007 lines · 9/9 tests

The plan was the jump from the state sum to matrices: represent each crossing as A·I + A−1·Ui, multiply along a braid word, and take a trace. The source keeps the attempt in a long comment, and it is worth reading as a record of working something out: tensor-product matrices, then path bases, then "U_1 acts as: [[delta, 1], [1, 0]] ... no.", then "OK, I'm going in circles", and finally a decision to do the honest thing first. What the demo actually builds is braid-word input to the same state sum: the closure is modelled as a grid of (crossings+1) × strands points, each smoothing either joins strands i and i+1 (a cup) or lets them pass, the tops are joined to the bottoms, and a union-find counts the loops. The one subtlety it records is that a positive and a negative crossing exchange which smoothing is the cup.

Unknot, trefoil (σ13), its mirror (σ1−3, the reversed polynomial), figure-eight and Hopf link all match demo 1. In the 2-strand case the matrix idea does work: the representation is one-dimensional and gives ρ(σ1) = −A−3 and ρ(σ1−1) = −A3, chirality as the exchange A ↔ A−1. The full Markov trace is deferred to demo 3. One test is left as an open note: σ1σ2σ1 on three strands, labelled as the trefoil, gives an f-polynomial different from σ13's, which the demo attributes to a missing Markov correction (see note 1).

Demo 03 · The Temperley–Lieb module

knotapel/demo_03/main.c · 671 lines (v3) · 13/13 tests

Demo 3 finds the matrices demo 2 was looking for. For three strands it derives, from the diagrams, the two-dimensional standard module W1 of the Temperley–Lieb algebra TL3 (basis: strands 1–2 capped with 3 free, or 2–3 capped with 1 free), where U1 = [[δ, 1], [0, 0]] and U2 = [[0, 0], [1, δ]], and it checks the algebra's relations exactly: Ui2 = δUi and U1U2U1 = U1. The header comment again shows the work: it expects a Hecke-algebra relation, computes that it fails ("AARGH. Let me look at this from first principles"), and then finds the simple truth that the inverse crossing is just the other smoothing, so (A·I + A−1U)(A−1·I + A·U) = I because A2 + A−2 + δ = 0.

With crossings as invertible 2×2 matrices of polynomials it verifies the braid relation g1g2g1 = g2g1g2, and then the payoff: the bracket of any 3-strand braid is a weighted sum of two traces, ⟨β⟩ = trW1(ρ(β)) + (A4 + A−4)·trW3(ρ(β)), where W3 is the one-dimensional module in which every U acts as 0. For the figure-eight, the 3-strand braid of demo 2, the mirror, a single crossing and the identity, this equals the state-sum oracle exactly. The exponential sum over smoothings has become a product of small matrices.

U_1 = [[δ, 1],      U_2 = [[0, 0],      g_i = A^-1·I + A·U_i
       [0, 0]]             [1, δ]]      g_i^-1 = A·I + A^-1·U_i

Demo 04 · One trace instead of two

knotapel/demo_04_spin_chain/main.c · 717 lines · 12/12 tests

Demo 3 needed two modules and a hand-chosen weight. Demo 4 asks whether a single space contains both: give each strand a two-dimensional space V, so three strands live in V⊗V⊗V (eight dimensions, a "spin chain"). The cup-cap becomes a 4×4 matrix on V⊗V built from the quantum singlet |01⟩ − A2|10⟩, whose self-pairing is exactly δ; the crossing is R = A·U + A−1·I, placed on strands 1–2 or 2–3 as R⊗I or I⊗R. The demo verifies U2 = δU, the inverse, and the Yang–Baxter equation R1R2R1 = R2R1R2 on the 8×8 matrices.

The open question was which trace gives the bracket, and the demo records the answer it found: a quantum trace, weighting each basis state by K = diag(A2, A−2) on every strand, divided by A2 + A−2. Why it works is the representation theory underneath (Schur–Weyl duality): V⊗V⊗V splits as V3/2⊗W3 (4 × 1 dimensions) plus V1/2⊗W1 (2 × 2), and the quantum trace weights each part by the quantum dimension of its V, which after the division is exactly demo 3's formula, weight A4 + A−4 included. Demo 3's hand-assembled sum turns out to be one trace in a bigger space.

Demo 05 · Any number of strands

knotapel/demo_05_scaling/main.c · 766 lines · 39/39 tests

Demo 5 asks whether the formula is a fact about three strands or about braids. It builds the generators Ri = I⊗(i−1) ⊗ R ⊗ I⊗(n−i−1) for n = 2, 3, 4 and 5 strands (matrices up to 32×32 whose entries are polynomials, now allocated on the heap), and checks for each n the braid relations: Yang–Baxter for neighbouring generators, and far commutativity (σ1σ3 = σ3σ1, which first exists at four strands). It also checks the quantum integers that the quantum dimensions are made of, both by their recurrence [n+1]q = [2]q[n]q − [n−1]q and by the closed form [n]q(A2 − A−2) = A2n − A−2n.

The quantum trace works at every n, with one correction found on the way: a sign that alternates with the number of strands, trq = (−1)n·δ·⟨β⟩. Demo 4 had only seen n = 3, where the sign is −1. The demo explains it from the identity braid: the quantum trace of the identity is (−δ)n, while the bracket of n unlinked circles is δn−1, so the ratio carries (−1)n. With that, the bracket of a braid on any number of strands is a matrix product and one weighted trace, exact over the integers, and every value agrees with the demo-1 oracle.

Demo 06 · Where the weights come from

knotapel/demo_06_jones_wenzl/main.c · 799 lines · 27/27 tests

Demos 3 and 4 found the bracket as a weighted sum of traces, with weights that "looked like magic numbers" (the file's own words). Demo 6 shows where they come from: the Jones–Wenzl idempotents pn, the projectors onto the highest-spin part of V⊗n. A projector is idempotent (p2 = p), every cup-cap kills it (Uipn = 0), and its quantum trace is a quantum integer. Written directly, pn has fractions of polynomials in it, so the demo keeps everything polynomial with a scaling trick: it builds Qn = Dn·pn, starting from Q2 = δ·I − U and growing Q3 from Q2 by the standard recursion, and checks Qn2 = DnQn, the annihilation, and trq(Qn) = Dn[n+1]q, exactly.

With Q3 in hand the quantum trace of any 3-strand braid splits in two: the part through the projector is exactly [4]q·trW3, the one-dimensional module's trace times its quantum dimension, checked for four braids. So demo 3's weight A4 + A−4 is [4]q/[2]q: a ratio of quantum dimensions, not a magic number. The other part, which should be [2]q·trW1, is checked more weakly, only for exact divisibility; the code says plainly that the sum of the two parts is tautological and that the W3 part carries the real content.

Demo 07 · From braids back to diagrams

knotapel/demo_07_braid_closure/main.c · 790 lines · 25/25 tests

Until here there were two worlds: diagrams in PD notation (demo 1's oracle) and braid words (everything since). Demo 7 builds the bridge, braid_to_pd: it follows each strand position through the crossings that touch it, numbers the arcs between them, and writes each crossing as its four arcs in PD order. The bracket of the generated diagram equals the braid's bracket for every test braid, including the chiral trefoil. The demo also counts Seifert circles (smooth every crossing the way that respects orientation) and finds, as theory says, one per strand of the braid; and it counts link components, which follow the braid's permutation.

Two of its findings matter for the earlier demos. The "obvious" counter-clockwise PD labelling produces the mirror image, which is why demo 1's trefoil came out left-handed; the fix is to swap two positions. And σ1σ2σ1 on three strands has two components, "not a trefoil", which answers demo 2's open note (see note 1). One limit is visible in the code: a strand that no crossing touches gets no arcs, so its circle would be missing from the diagram; no test braid has such a strand.

Demo 08 · From diagrams to braids

knotapel/demo_08_vogel/main.c · 886 lines · 47/47 tests

The other direction, which its README calls the compiler: given a PD diagram, produce a braid whose closure is the same knot. The demo finds the Seifert circles with a union-find, builds the graph of which circles each crossing joins, and when that graph is a simple chain, the circles become the braid's strands in chain order and each crossing becomes a generator between two neighbouring strands. The hard part is the order of the crossings. Each circle visits its crossings in a cyclic order; the demo tries every choice of where to cut each cycle, turns the result into a precedence graph, and accepts the first choice that sorts topologically. The insight it records: crossings between the same pair of circles commute, so no order is imposed between them. Without that, the trefoil, whose three crossings all join the same two circles, looks contradictory.

The trefoil, figure-eight and Hopf link compile to σ13, σ1σ2−1σ1σ2−1 and σ12, and round trips (diagram to braid to diagram, twice) preserve the bracket. The name promises a little more than the code does: Vogel's algorithm is the set of moves that turn any diagram's Seifert circles into a chain; this demo handles the case where they already form one and refuses the rest (the README says so), and the search over cut points grows exponentially with the size of the diagram.

Demo 09 · The moves that don't change a knot

knotapel/demo_09_reidemeister/main.c · 716 lines · 28/28 tests

Two diagrams show the same knot exactly when they are connected by Reidemeister moves: R1 adds or removes a kink, R2 slides one strand over another (two crossings), R3 moves a strand across a crossing. Demo 9 writes R1 and R2 as operations on PD codes (split an arc, add the new arcs and crossings) and checks what the theory says: R1 multiplies the bracket by −A3 or −A−3 and changes the writhe by one, so the writhe-normalized f-polynomial is unchanged; R2 changes nothing. R3 is tested through the braid relation σ1σ2σ1 = σ2σ1σ2, including inside a longer 4-strand braid, and combinations of moves leave f unchanged. This is the demo that says the bracket, once normalized, is a knot invariant and not just a property of a drawing.

One caution in the code: the R2 helper accepts any two arcs and does not check that they border the same region of the diagram, so the result may not be drawable in the plane. The bracket identity it checks holds either way (the R2 cancellation is local to the two new crossings), so the tests prove the algebra, not that each poked diagram is a real one.

Demo 10 · From polynomials to numbers

knotapel/demo_10_numerical/main.c · 955 lines · 108/108 tests

Exact polynomials don't scale: a 32×32 matrix of them was about 800 KB. Demo 10 fixes A = eiθ and turns every polynomial into one complex number, so the same R-matrices and quantum trace become ordinary complex linear algebra. It checks the complex arithmetic, the inverses and the Yang–Baxter equation numerically, matches the state-sum oracle (itself evaluated at the same A) for every test braid at several angles, and scales to eight strands (256×256 matrices), where far commutativity and the braid relations still hold to 10−9. It is the one step in these ten demos that uses floating point; the project later made "avoid floating point" a rule, and the computing results moved to exact arithmetic in Z[ζ8].

It ends with the experiment that points toward computation: how many sample angles are needed to tell a table of knots apart? At θ = π/5 a single angle separates all six. The result is true of the six braids, but the table's names are mostly wrong: only the figure-eight is the knot named, and the others are links (see note 3).

Demo 11 · The bracket as a waveform

knotapel/demo_11_waveform/main.c · 648 lines · 16/16 tests

Demo 10 sampled the bracket at single points; demo 11 sweeps A around the whole unit circle (256 samples) and treats each braid's bracket as a complex waveform, with an amplitude and a phase. The prediction it tests is elegant: on the unit circle A−1 is the complex conjugate of A, and mirroring a knot replaces A by A−1, so a knot and its mirror image must have identical amplitude and differ only in phase; an amphichiral knot like the figure-eight must be symmetric. Both hold to machine precision. The demo then searches for the angle that best separates its ten entries, finding θ ≈ 1.805π, where one angle alone separates every pair with a margin of 0.907, and draws the amplitude waveforms as ASCII bands.

The table grows to ten entries and fixes one of demo 10's names: the granny is now σ13σ23, a real connected sum, joined by the square knot σ13σ2−3. The "trefoil", "torus(2,5)", "torus(2,7)" and "cinquefoil mirror" entries are still the alternating words that close to two-component links (note 4). The mirror-symmetry results stand regardless, since every mirror pair is a word and its inverse.

Demo 12 · How many bits is a knot?

knotapel/demo_12_quantization/main.c · 779 lines · 7/7 tests

If a knot is one complex number at a well-chosen angle, how coarsely can that number be stored and still tell the table apart? Demo 12 quantizes the real and imaginary parts to 16, 12, 8, 6, 4, 3, 2 and 1 bits, with an eye on 1.58-bit "BitNet" language models. At one angle, 4 bits per component (8 bits per knot) keeps all 45 pairs apart, and 3 bits loses one. It also splits the signal: amplitude alone fails, as demo 11 predicted, because mirror pairs, and the granny and square knot, share their amplitude; phase alone also fails; sign-only and ternary codes separate 33 and 42 of 45. A precision-by-width table finds the cheapest combination, 2 bits at 2 angles, again 8 bits per knot, and a nearest-neighbour classifier recognizes every entry and two re-spelled braids.

The question is a good one and the framing (a compiled weight table) is where the project is heading. What this demo measures is how well quantized values keep these ten braids apart; the "re-spelled" test braids are the same braid group elements written differently (σ2σ1σ2 for σ1σ2σ1, and a cyclic rotation), so recognizing them shows the pipeline is consistent rather than that it generalizes.

Demo 13 · Does it generalize?

knotapel/demo_13_dkc_test/main.c · 948 lines · 7/7 tests

Demo 13 is the first one to carry the name DKC, here expanded as "Deterministic Knowledge Compilation", and it asks the critical question: do weights compiled from the bracket generalize, the way trained weights are supposed to? It builds a catalog of 28 three-strand braids (six torus families with their mirrors, eight connected sums, six other braids), merges entries whose brackets agree at two angles (28 become 23), and then measures separation, the bits-versus-angles frontier (now 16 bits per knot for 23 entries, up from 8 for 10), a train/test split, a random baseline, and how the closest pair's distance shrinks as the table grows.

Read closely, the demo does not yet answer its question. The catalog's torus "knots" are built by a formula that produces links (note 4); merging by agreement at two angles is not a test of knot type; and the train/test and random-baseline parts contain checks that cannot fail, so "generalization" and "beats random" are not actually measured (note 7). What it does show is that 23 different bracket signatures stay apart at one angle and how the margin decays. The real generalization result came later and differently: demo 29 computes XOR from exact bracket values with no training at all.

Demo 14 · Where the bracket vanishes

knotapel/demo_14_zero_structure/main.c · 830 lines · 7/7 tests

Demo 11's waveforms had zeros, and demo 14 asks whether the zeros are an invariant: it locates them at 1,024 samples with a refining search, counts them, maps their positions, and asks how much of the table they classify. The zeros fall into a few exact patterns, four at the odd multiples of π/4 or eight at the odd multiples of π/8, mirror pairs share them in reflected positions, and there is a clean gap (a ratio of about 1012) between entries whose bracket truly vanishes and entries that never come near zero. Zero counts alone separate 128 of 210 pairs.

Two of its conclusions do not survive a check. The "torus knot zero progression" (8, 0, 0, 8, 4, 4) is a pattern in the links of note 4. And its connected-sum test compares the granny with the square of its "trefoil", finds a large mismatch, and concludes "braid connected sum ≠ diagram connected sum (expected)". With the real trefoil the bracket is exactly multiplicative: the demo's own granny values equal the trefoil's bracket squared at every angle it prints (note 5). The claim that every zero is a zero of δ is also not right for the eight-zero pattern (note 6).

Demo 15 · Looking for the special angles

knotapel/demo_15_angle_optimization/main.c · 883 lines · 8/8 tests

Demo 15 starts from a striking coincidence: the angle the greedy search picked, 1.805π, is within 1.5% of 11π/6 = 1.833π, the point where the bracket meets the Witten–Reshetikhin–Turaev invariant at level r = 3 (through the Jones variable t = A−4). If an optimizer had rediscovered a point that Chern–Simons theory singles out, that would say the algebraically special angles are the informative ones. The demo states five predictions, computes the WRT angles for six levels between r = 3 and r = 12, divides out the factor of δ it believes demo 14 found to get a "reduced bracket" g, and compares separation at every candidate angle.

The numbers come out against the hypothesis, and the demo reports them: the r = 3 angle separates only 164 of 210 pairs, with some pairs at distance zero, while the greedy angle separates all of them; the best WRT angle (r = 12) reaches a hardest-pair distance of 0.196 against the greedy angle's 0.443. The code's comment draws the sober conclusion, that WRT points are algebraically special, not separation-optimal. The checks were written to assert the observed outcome rather than the stated predictions (note 7), and the reduced bracket rests on a δ-factor that is only partly there (note 6). This is the end of the numerical-classification line; from demo 16 the project moves toward computing with brackets rather than classifying with them.

Demo 16 · What is the reduced bracket?

knotapel/demo_16_reduced_bracket/main.c · 974 lines · 9/9 tests

Demo 15 had divided each bracket by its power of δ to get a "reduced bracket" g. Demo 16 characterizes g: its waveform at 256 samples (no more zeros than the raw bracket), the shape of its best angle near 1.266π (a plateau about 0.09π wide, not a spike, so the angle is robust), its bit frontier (6 bits per component against the raw bracket's 7), whether it factorizes under connected sum, and the smallest set of angles a greedy search needs. At one angle g's hardest pair is twice as far apart as the raw bracket's (1.90 against 0.94).

The factorization part concludes that g does not factorize, "seven of seven" composites off by large factors, and the code's comment calls this structural, confirming demo 14. It is demo 14's mistake again, comparing against the Hopf-link "trefoil", and the check records it with a constant pass (note 8). The angle-set part needs a caution too: its greedy search may pick the same angle again, and does, so its "20 angles" for g are three distinct angles.

Demo 17 · Does a hierarchy beat a flat table?

knotapel/demo_17_multilayer_dkc/main.c · 1,467 lines · 6/6 tests

Demo 17 asks an architecture question: instead of one flat lookup at one angle, would a decision tree do better, first splitting the table by a cheap feature and then using a different angle for each group? It builds a flat baseline, a two-group split by the δ-exponent k (which it calls the Arf invariant, noting that neural networks reportedly cannot learn Arf, so here it would come "for free"), a greedy binary tree of angle splits, and a gradient-descent search for the per-group angles to compare with the analytic choice. The analytic angles match gradient descent (2.000 against 1.99997), and the tree classifies the table at 3 bits per value against the flat table's 6.

Two corrections. The split bit is not the Arf invariant: k records whether the bracket vanishes where δ does, and for a knot it never can, so every genuine knot in the table has k = 0 and the "free" bit only sets five links apart (note 9). And the bit comparison counts bits per stored value only: the split costs an extra bit and the tree eleven decision bits plus an angle per leaf, so the stated prediction that hierarchy saves bits is not tested in total bits; the arf split ties the flat table (6 against 6) and the check passes on the tie.

Demo 18 · Logic gates from braids

knotapel/demo_18_braid_logic/main.c · 1,037 lines · 7/7 tests

Can braids compute classical logic? Demo 18 encodes a bit as "no crossing" (0) or "one crossing" σi (1), prepends the inputs to a fixed gate braid, takes the magnitude of the bracket at some angle, and thresholds it. It searches every gate braid up to six crossings on two strands for a NOT and up to five on three strands for a NAND, and finds both: NOT = σ1−6 at 1.25π (outputs 6 and 5), NAND = σ1−3σ22 at 1.75π (outputs 6, 9, 4 and 0), and an XOR as well. Both work over a good fraction of all angles (37% and 44%).

What a "gate" means here matters. Its output is a real number compared with a threshold outside the braid; its inputs are crossings. The demo's own composition test shows the consequence: feeding NAND into NOT does not give AND at NAND's angle, and the AND it then reports is found by sweeping for a different angle. So the summary line "UNIVERSAL: YES — NOT + NAND = Turing complete" claims more than the demo shows (note 10). The finding that survives is real and interesting: simple braids have brackets whose magnitudes separate a truth table cleanly.

Demo 19 · A compiler, and the special root

knotapel/demo_19_braid_compiler/main.c · 1,016 lines · 13/13 tests

Demo 19 tries demo 18's gates at the roots of unity of order 4, 6, 8, 10 and 12. NOT works at many of them; NAND only at four of the 8th roots and four of the 10th, and only at the 8th roots do both work at the same angle: the odd ones, θ = π/4, 3π/4, 5π/4, 7π/4, exactly where δ = 0. There the gates work with integer outputs and the reduced bracket equals the raw one. It fixes A = ei5π/4 and builds a small Boolean expression compiler on top: expressions are trees of NOT and NAND, AND, OR and XOR are derived, and a full adder (sum and carry, all eight input combinations) comes out right, at about 2 ms per evaluation and a crossing count that grows linearly with depth.

The compiler is honest about how it composes: each gate's output is thresholded to an ordinary bit in C, and that bit selects the next gate's input braid. The circuit's wiring therefore lives outside the braids; each braid acts as a lookup table for one gate. The lasting result is the root: A = ei5π/4, where every diagram with more than one loop contributes nothing and the bracket takes exact integer-like values, is the point the whole later DKC program is built on.

Demo 20 · Where the gates stop working

knotapel/demo_20_braid_error/main.c · 1,016 lines · 7/7 tests

At δ = 0 the gates are exact; demo 20 asks what happens as A moves away. It verifies |δ| = 2|cos 2θ| across 360 angles (zero exactly at the four odd 8th roots), sweeps NOT and NAND around 5π/4 to find where each fails (NOT at |δ| ≈ 0.59, NAND at ≈ 1.05), runs the full adder across the sweep, tabulates gate success by |δ| alone (NAND works throughout |δ| < 1 and nowhere in 1 ≤ |δ| < 1.75), and finds the transition gradual rather than sharp. That is a useful map: the computing works in a band around δ = 0, not only at a point.

One conclusion does not follow: "cascade does NOT amplify", because the full adder fails later than the NAND gate alone. The adder passes at exactly the angles where NOT fails and NAND still works, and the reason is in the circuit, not the braids (note 11).

Demo 21 · Reversible gates

knotapel/demo_21_reversible_gates/main.c · 857 lines · 6/6 tests

Demo 21 builds the two classic reversible gates, Toffoli (flip C when A and B) and Fredkin (swap B and C when A), from demo 19's compiler, and gets both truth tables right. Applying either twice gives the identity, as reversible gates must. It then asks a new question: one braid can be closed in four ways (the trace closure, or "plat" closures that cap off different strands), so does one braid compute several functions at once? Yes: every one of 1,554 four-strand braids up to four crossings gives more than one truth table across its closures, up to four distinct ones. None of them directly computes a Toffoli or Fredkin output.

Two of its comparisons are softer than they look. "Double Toffoli = identity" is a property of the Toffoli function, so it follows from the correct truth table rather than adding evidence about braids. And the crossing count, 58 against about 132 for a quantum Toffoli braided from coloured anyons, compares eleven separate classical evaluations with one quantum braid; the demo says so itself ("not apples-to-apples").

Demo 24 · Rational tangles

knotapel/demo_24_rational_tangles/main.c · 889 lines · 6/7 tests (one fails as shipped)

A tangle with two strands in and two out can be twisted (add a crossing) or rotated a quarter turn; Conway showed these two moves build exactly the rational tangles, one for each fraction p/q, assembled like a continued fraction. Demo 24 represents a tangle's bracket in TL2 as two coefficients, writes twisting and rotation as operations on them, and evaluates at δ = 0. The demo undersells itself twice (note 12). It reports that the fraction is tracked for only 8 of 14 rationals; in fact all 14 match, once the expected value is computed for what twist-and-rotate builds (a continued fraction with minus signs). Demo 25 · Closures as measurements

Demo 26 · Do trained weights look like brackets?

Demo 26 runs the experiment the project calls reverse DKC. At δ = 0 the catalog turns out to be the integers: magnitudes 0 to 16, missing only 10, 14 and 15. The project's own summaries quote "weights approximate bracket values, RMS ≈ 0.26" as a result; it is the expected rounding error.

Demo 29 · Bracket values as weights, no training

knotapel/demo_29_cyclotomic_arithmetic/main.c · 903 lines · 24/24 tests

Demo 29 is the one the project marks as its landmark: forward DKC. It implements exact arithmetic in Z[ζ8] (a value is four integers; a product is sixteen integer multiplications; no floating point), recomputes the bracket of all 8,192 braids on two and three strands up to eight crossings exactly (every value agrees with the floating-point version), and finds 64 distinct values. Then it takes every triple of them as the two weights and bias of a complex neuron, tries global phases, and asks which triples compute XOR. It also shows the bracket of a product of braids is not the product of brackets (it is a trace, not a homomorphism).

The existence result is solid, and exact. The size of the result needs correcting (note 16): the reported 65,536 "valid triples" is the size of the array that stores them, which fills partway through the search; uncapped, 28,644 of the 262,144 triples (10.9%) work at some phase. Demo 35 · Exact Temperley–Lieb matrices for any n

Demo 38 · How much of the algebra the bracket uses

knotapel/demo_38_dimension_reduction/main.c · 781 lines · 13/13 tests

At δ = 0 the Temperley–Lieb algebra is not semisimple for even n: part of it, the radical, is invisible to every trace, so the bracket never sees it. Demo 38 measures how much. It builds TLn for n = 2 to 8 (up to 1,430 dimensions) on the planar-matching basis, computes the rank of the trace form exactly (modulo a large prime), and finds the pattern its header states: for odd n the form has full rank Cn (semisimple), for even n its rank drops to Cn−1 (5 of 14 at n = 4, 42 of 132 at n = 6, 429 of 1,430 at n = 8). So bracket computation at even n needs only a Cn−1-dimensional quotient, about a quarter of the size; the demo builds reduced generators on it and checks they satisfy the TL relations. It also sorts the basis by the number of strands that pass straight through, and finds the 0-through sector entirely dead at even n.

Two of its outputs are left hanging. The quotient is compared with TLn−1 only by counting nonzero products in their multiplication tables (17 and 17 at n = 4), which agrees with an isomorphism but does not establish one. And at n = 6 and 8 the per-sector ranks add up to more than the total (46 against 42, 533 against 429), printed without comment. Demo 39 takes that one up.

Demo 39 · The algebra's sectors

knotapel/demo_39_symmetry_decomposition/main.c · 1,720 lines · 31/31 tests

Demo 39 is the careful follow-up. It checks that each through-strand sector has size d(n,j)2, the square of a cell module's dimension (the cellular structure of Graham and Lehrer); measures the cross-sector coupling that demo 38 left unexplained (none at n = 4, positive at n = 6, where the sector ranks overcount the total); verifies the cell filtration (the number of through-strands never increases under multiplication); compares δ = 0 with δ = 1 and δ = φ (TL4 is non-semisimple at 1, semisimple at the golden ratio, as theory predicts); shows the generators are block lower-triangular, not block-diagonal, even where the algebra is semisimple; and compares three different Gram forms at n = ℓ, where the cell modules detect non-semisimplicity as Graham–Lehrer say they should.

Demo 45 · Thirteen functions, one ranking

knotapel/demo_45_complex_function_zoo/main.c · 1,387 lines · 9/11 tests (two fail as shipped) · findings.md, informal-debrief.md

Demo 45 extends reverse DKC from XOR to every class of three-input Boolean function. The ranking is real, but it has a simpler explanation (note 17). Notes from this reading (2026-10-03)

Each was checked by hand algebra or by running the code; none changes a test result, since every test compares like with like. I have not edited anything in knotapel/; recording these in the project's own log or atlas is the project's call.

  1. σ1σ2σ1 is a Hopf link, not a trefoil. Demo 2 leaves it as an open note that σ1σ2σ1 on three strands, labelled the trefoil, disagrees with σ13. Its permutation swaps strands 1 and 3 and fixes strand 2, so the closure has two components, which demo 7 itself discovered ("a 2-component link (not a trefoil)"). It is the Hopf link with one extra stabilizing crossing: its bracket is A + A−7 = (−A−3)(−A4 − A−4), and with the writhe factor whose sign matches these demos' crossing convention ((−A3)+w), σ12 and σ1σ2σ1 both give −A2 − A10: Markov invariance held. The label survives in demos 3 and 6 ("Trefoil (3-strand)", "trefoil") and in demo 10's table; demos 3 and 6 compare against the state sum of the same braid, so their tests stand. The trefoil as a 3-strand braid is (σ1σ2)2.

  2. Demo 1's printed f-polynomial is mis-normalized. The trefoil's PD code labels all three crossings positive (writhe +3), but under the demo's smoothing convention the diagram is left-handed (demo 7 explains the labelling cause). Normalizing a left-handed bracket with w = +3 gives A−14 + A−6 − A−2, which the test accepts as "the left-handed f" because its expected value was derived the same way. With the writhe that matches the bracket (−3) the value is A4 + A12 − A16. That is exactly what the atlas's demo index lists for demo 1, labelled there as the bracket: the atlas has the right f-polynomial under the wrong name, and the demo the wrong one under the right name. Demo 2's f-values share the convention mismatch.

  3. Demo 10's knot table is mostly links. Counting the components of each braid's closure: "unknot" (the identity on three strands) is the 3-component unlink, whose bracket is δ2 (0.381966 at θ = π/5, as printed); "trefoil" and "mirror trefoil" (σ1σ2σ1 and its inverse) are Hopf links; "torus(2,5)" (σ1σ2σ1σ2σ1) has two components; "granny" ((σ1σ2σ1)2, the full twist) is the 3-component (3,3) torus link. Only the figure-eight is a knot. The real ones on three strands would be the trefoil (σ1σ2)2, T(2,5) σ15σ2 and the granny σ13σ23. Also, the experiment compares raw brackets, which depend on the diagram's framing, so it separates braid diagrams rather than knot types. "One angle suffices for six" stands as a statement about these six braids. Demos 11 to 13 build their knot tables on this one; the labels did carry over (note 4).

  4. The catalogs of demos 11 to 15 build links where they name torus knots. Demo 13's helper add_torus_2n builds T(2,n) on three strands as the alternating word (σ1σ2)(n−1)/2σ1 (its comment gives that formula), and demos 11 and 12 use the same words. Every one of them closes to a two-component link: counting each braid's permutation cycles, all twelve "T(2,n)" entries of demo 13 (n = 3 to 13, with mirrors) have two components, as do "T23#fig8" and the unnamed σ12σ2 and σ12σ2−1 (σ12σ2±2σ12 have three), and the "unknot" is again the three-component unlink. The torus knot T(2,n) on three strands is σ1nσ2. The connected sums built from σ1aσ2b (granny, square, T23#T25 …) and the figure-eight are knots, as named; "T23#fig8" is not a connected sum, because a figure-eight needs both generators. Results that only compare entries with each other (separations, symmetries, bit depths) remain true of these braids; results stated about torus knots are about these links. The same catalog continues into demos 16 and 17.

  5. The bracket is multiplicative under connected sum; demo 14's opposite conclusion comes from the labels. Demo 14 tests ⟨granny⟩ against ⟨"trefoil"⟩2/δ and finds a mismatch. Its "trefoil" is the Hopf link σ1σ2σ1, and in this normalization (one circle = 1) the rule has no δ. Against the real trefoil (σ13, bracket −A−5 − A3 + A7), the granny's values printed by the demo, (2.6180, −3.8042) at 0.3π, (2.6180, 3.8042) at 0.7π, (0.3820, −2.3511) at 1.1π and (−1, 0) at 1.5π, are exactly ⟨trefoil⟩2 at the same angles. The check "braid connected sum ≠ diagram connected sum (expected)" passes on a false premise.

  6. Not every zero is a zero of δ. δ = −2cos 2θ vanishes at the odd multiples of π/4, which is where the four-zero entries vanish (the three-circle unlink is δ2). The eight-zero entries vanish at the odd multiples of π/8 instead: the "trefoil" (the stabilized Hopf link) has bracket A−7(A8 + 1), zero exactly where A8 = −1. So demo 14's "all zero positions are at universal angles (delta zeros)" and demo 15's premise that "g never vanishes" do not hold for those entries; demo 15's own δ-exponent finds k = 0 for them, leaving their eight zeros in g. Its δ-exponent also assigns k = 1 to the unlink, whose bracket is δ2: the second-order test divides the amplitude near π/4 by δ there, gets about 0.004 (which is just δ again), and compares it with a threshold of 0.001.

  7. Some checks in demos 13 to 15 cannot fail. In demo 13, a test item counts as correctly classified if it is not identical to a training item, which deduplication already guaranteed, and that count is printed but not asserted; the asserted checks are self-classification of the training set and a re-spelled σ1σ2σ1. Its random baseline separates 100% of pairs, and "DKC beats random baseline (100.0% vs 100%)" passes because the check ignores the baseline. In demo 14, "count+positions improves over count alone (128 > 128)" passes with no improvement (the check is ≥). Demo 15 states as predictions that the WRT angles match or beat the greedy angle; the measurements show the opposite, and the checks assert the opposite ("greedy outperforms best WRT"), so the predictions are refuted by the numbers while the run reports 8/8. The 1.5% coincidence that motivated it is one of several WRT angles (r = 3 to 12 lie between 1.83π and 1.96π) near a greedy optimum, which is weak evidence on its own.

  8. Demo 16 repeats demo 14's connected-sum mistake. Its factorization test pairs each composite with its "factors" from the same catalog, so the granny is compared with the square of the Hopf-link "trefoil". The check is check(msg, 1), a constant pass that records the result, and the comment concludes "the issue is structural". With the real trefoil the bracket factorizes exactly (note 5); for these knots k = 0, so g is the bracket and factorizes too. Two smaller points: the greedy angle search adds an angle's squared distances again if it picks the same angle twice, which raises its score, so it does: the g search chooses 0.25π fifteen times and 1.25π four times out of twenty, and the raw search 0.25π seven times. And at the exact zeros of δ, where g is undefined, the code returns the raw bracket instead, so g is discontinuous at the very angles the greedy search keeps choosing.

  9. The δ-exponent is not the Arf invariant. k counts whether the bracket vanishes at the zeros of δ, which is the Jones variable t = A−4 = −1. There |VK(−1)| is the determinant of the knot, and a knot's determinant is always odd, so for a knot the bracket never vanishes there: k = 0 for every knot, which is what the output shows (the figure-eight, granny, square and all the composites). The five entries with k > 0 are links: the three-circle unlink and four of the mislabelled "torus knots". The Arf invariant belongs to the evaluation at t = i instead (VK(i) = (−1)Arf(K)), and by a theorem of Murasugi a link's V(i) vanishes exactly when the link is not "proper". That is the 8-zero pattern of note 6: the Hopf links vanish at the odd multiples of π/8, which is where t = ±i. So the project had the Arf evaluation in front of it, in demo 14's zero table, under another name.

  10. A thresholded bracket is a lookup table, not yet a gate. In demos 18 and 19 a gate's inputs are crossings and its output is a real number compared with a threshold chosen afterwards. Nothing inside the braid passes a bit on; demo 18's composition test shows this (NAND then NOT does not give AND at NAND's angle), and demo 19 composes by thresholding each gate's output in C and choosing the next braid from the resulting bit. So each braid is a precomputed truth table and the circuit is ordinary code. "NOT + NAND = Turing complete" also overstates: that pair is functionally complete for Boolean circuits, which is a different and weaker property. The Freedman–Kitaev–Wang universality the demo cites concerns quantum computation with unitary braid representations, not thresholded bracket magnitudes. The later DKC demos took a different and stronger route: bracket values used as neuron weights.

  11. Demo 20's full adder survives because its NOTs cancel. The circuit builds AND as NOT(NAND) and OR as NAND(NOT, NOT), so the carry, OR(AND(a,b), AND(c, XOR)), becomes NAND(NOT(NOT(NAND(a,b))), NOT(NOT(NAND(c, XOR)))): every NOT sits in a cancelling pair, and the sum uses NANDs only. When the NOT gate degrades, its outputs at the midpoint threshold invert, so the "NOT" becomes a plain buffer, the pairs still cancel, and the adder stays correct. The table shows exactly this: the adder gets all eight cases right at θ = 0.800π, 0.951π, 1.051π and 1.202π, all points where NOT fails and NAND holds, and fails wherever NAND fails. So "cascade does NOT amplify" is a property of this circuit's double negations, not evidence of robustness.

  12. Demo 24 tracks all fourteen fractions, and its failing test is a mirror convention. Twisting adds one (x → x + 1) and rotating gives x → −1/x, so assembling [a0; a1, …, an] with those moves builds a0 − 1/(a1 − 1/(… − 1/an)), a continued fraction with minus signs, not the ordinary one the demo expects. Recomputed that way, the six "failures" all match: [0,1,2] gives −2 (the demo measures 2), [0,1,1,2] gives 1 (1), [0,1,1,1,2] gives −1/2 (0.5), [0,2,3] gives −3/5 (0.6), and likewise [0,1,1,1,1,2] (2) and [0,1,1,1,3] (1/3). So the bracket at δ = 0 reads off the tangle's fraction in every case, up to sign, in line with the theorem of Kauffman and Lambropoulou that the bracket at this root computes a rational tangle's fraction. The failing test P1b compares the TL2 coefficients with a state-sum oracle. Its errors follow the mirror image exactly: with A ↔ A−1, αn = An differs from A−n by 0 when n ≡ 0 (mod 4), 2 when n ≡ 2, and √2 when n is odd, and βn = nAn−2 matches its mirror exactly when n ≡ 2 (mod 4), which is the printed pattern (N errors 1.41, 2.00, 1.41, 0 …; D errors 0 at n = 2, 6, 10). It is the same chirality mismatch as notes 2 and 7: no magnitude differs.

  13. Demo 25's thresholds were set after the result. Its check P8 says "HW explains variance" with an F-ratio of 0.69, but the demo's own printout explains that F much greater than 1 would mean Hamming weight organizes the outputs; 0.69 is below 1, the opposite. The code's comment records the change: "F-ratio > 0.5 shows HW has explanatory power … Original threshold too aggressive." (The ratio is also a plain ratio of summed variances, without the degrees-of-freedom normalization of an F statistic.) P2 calls the closures "complementary" because the smallest mutual information is under 0.5 bits; five of the six pairs are above it.

  14. Cost comparisons in demos 21 and 22 compare unlike things. Demo 21's 58 crossings for a Toffoli are eleven separate classical evaluations, against a single quantum braid of about 132; demo 22's braid weight counts the gate braid but not the input crossings or the threshold, against a NAND expression's full crossing count. The figures show that short braids can encode functions; they are not measurements of relative efficiency.

  15. Matching weights to an integer catalog measures rounding. At δ = 0 every bracket magnitude is an integer (demo 26's catalog: 0 to 16 without 10, 14, 15). The distance from any real number to the nearest integer is spread evenly over [0, ½], so the RMS error of rounding is 1/√12 ≈ 0.2887 whatever the numbers are; the four converged networks of demo 26 average 0.2890 (0.316, 0.264, 0.307, 0.269). The random baseline is higher (0.368) because random weights drawn from [3, 12] fall into the catalog's gap around 10 while the trained ones happen not to, and the "1.39× better" compares the best trained network with the average random one. The "syndrome" label is guaranteed: it calls residuals "STRUCTURED" when their standard deviation is under 0.5, and rounding residuals always lie in [−½, ½]. The braid circuit and the network agreeing on XOR is true of any two correct XOR implementations. Demo 27's degeneracy check counts a weight with no complex match at all as a "99.9× reduction". Demos 27 and 28 deserve credit for shipping their failed predictions as failing tests.

  16. Demo 29: the count is capped, and a control is needed. Solutions are stored in an array of MAX_VALID = 65,536 entries, and the search stops recording when it is full; the progress lines show the count frozen at 65,536 from the 40th of 64 first-weight values on. Each entry is a (triple, phase) pair, and the phase sweep is 72 angles in 5° steps, not the 360 printed. Counted without the cap (a patched copy, the demo itself untouched): 200,933 (triple, phase) pairs and 28,644 distinct triples, 10.9% of all 262,144. The catalog itself is capped too: MAX_CATALOG = 8,192 braids, and "8,192 braids with |bracket| > 0.5" is that cap; enumerated completely (demo 113), the same catalog definition has 100 distinct values, not 64, so the triple counts above are for demo 29's truncated 64. The demo's "10,357 unique triples" was computed from the capped prefix; the project's summaries quote "65,536 valid triples" and "~35% of triples". For the control: the neuron reads out p = hre(1 − him) + (1 − hre)him, a soft XOR of two half-plane decisions, so it computes XOR whenever the real and imaginary parts realize two different threshold functions (for example OR and AND), which is Nitta's mechanism. Replacing the 64 bracket values by 64 points with the same magnitudes and random phases (three seeds) gives 35,248 to 37,889 working triples, 13.4% to 14.5%. Bracket values compute XOR without training, which is the demo's claim and is true; that they are unusually good at it is not supported. Later demos change the neuron (multi-valued activations, sector counts) and may well find structure this test cannot see; this reading covers only demo 29; note 20 extends the control to demo 50.

  17. Demo 45's correlation has a common cause: linear separability. Testing each class for a single-threshold realization (integer weights −4 to 4, every threshold) separates the ranking cleanly: BUF, AND2, AND3, A∨(B∧C) and MAJ, the five linearly separable classes, are ranks 1 to 5 (RMS 0.53 to 0.66); classes 0x19, 0x18, 0x1B, 0x06, XOR2, A⊕(B∨C), EXACT1 and parity, none separable, are ranks 6 to 13 (0.68 to 1.00). Demo 23's census assigns each braid the truth table of a threshold on |bracket|, so threshold functions dominate it as well. The two measurements use different code and methods, but not an independent subject: both measure how far a function is from a threshold function, so "completely independent measurements … no reason to agree" does not hold, and the correlation is not evidence about bracket values. Three further points. Both findings.md and the debrief rest on demo 27 having reached "RMS < 0.1 with gauge correction"; it reached 0.349, and its test for < 0.1 fails. The reversal of P5 (predicted r < 0, measured +0.87) is reinterpreted as "the deepest insight from the whole experiment", the post-hoc rationalization the project's own CLAUDE.md warns against. And P7, "gauge angles cluster at π/4 multiples", counts angles within ±15% of the π/4 spacing, which a random angle hits 30% of the time; 5 of 9 has a chance probability near 0.10. Each class is also represented by one trained network (the fastest of ten), and demos 26 and 27 showed this RMS moving from 0.19 to 0.32 between runs.

  18. Demos 46 and 47 measure chance in two places, and the baseline is not matched. Magnitude-only matching rounds each weight's size to the integer catalog (demo 113); random numbers give RMS 1/√12 ≈ 0.289, and demo 46's average is 0.33 (demo 47: 0.36, 0.40). The phase error (distance to the nearest multiple of π/4) of uniformly random phases averages π/16 ≈ 0.196 rad; demo 47 measures 0.189, 0.197 and 0.207 for its three activations. The random baseline (RMS 0.8086) is computed from 1,000 points with magnitudes |N(0,1)|·2 + 0.5, whatever the trained networks' magnitudes, and without the phase sweep the trained weights get; "13 of 13 beat random" therefore compares a fitted match with an unfitted one at a different scale. The rank correlations (n = 9) move from −0.78 to +0.55 to −0.10 with the activation, which is what an unstable statistic does. The "bits per symbol" figures in the findings' lens sections are not measured.

  19. Demo 48's parity wall is the neuron's. The readout p = σ(Re z)(1 − σ(Im z)) + (1 − σ(Re z))σ(Im z) satisfies p − ½ = −2(σ(Re z) − ½)(σ(Im z) − ½), so p > ½ exactly when Re z and Im z have opposite signs: the output is [Re z > 0] XOR [Im z > 0], the XOR of two threshold functions of the inputs (Re z and Im z are real linear functions of them). Enumerating the 104 threshold functions of three inputs (the known count): no pair XORs to parity, and the XORs of pairs cover all 254 other functions. So with this neuron parity is unreachable for every weight set and every other class is reachable for generic weights, before any bracket is computed; the radical is not needed to explain it. Demo 49 then reaches the correct diagnosis (the activation), and demo 50 acts on it.

  20. Demo 50 against null catalogs. A scratch copy of demo 50 (the demo untouched) replaced its 100 bracket values after building the catalog, three seeds each: shuffle, the same magnitudes re-paired at random with the same directions (breaking each knot's determinant–phase pairing); star, the same directions with random integer magnitudes 1 to 45; generic, random elements of Z[ζ8] with coefficients −3 to 3; randphase, the same magnitudes with continuous random phases. Parity solutions for k = 2 … 8: bracket 0 0 0 0 906 756 96; shuffle 0 0 0 0 762–1,242, 348–576, 6–34; star 0 0 0 0 714–1,368, 90–186, 6–12; generic 0 0 0 0 1,682–2,918, 2,082–2,820, 2,642–10,138; randphase 0 0 0 0 678–1,218, 1,452–2,322, 1,692–4,302. Every catalog first reaches parity at k = 6 and none below, so the six-sector threshold belongs to the activation and three-input parity; the decline after k = 6 belongs to the eight directions (it appears for star and shuffle, not for generic or randphase), which demo 113 traces to the writhe phase. The bracket catalog behaves like a typical eight-direction star of integers.

Demo 113 · Rosetta (new, from this reading)

knotapel/demo_113_rosetta/main.c · 33/33 tests · findings.md · written 2026-10-03

The first experiment below, built. Classically, ⟨L⟩ = (−A3)wVL(t) with t = A−4; at A = ei5π/4 that is t = −1, where |VL(−1)| is the determinant and VL(−1) is real or imaginary as the number of components is odd or even. The demo states six predictions in its header before running, then tests them on every braid word on 2 strands up to length 12, 3 strands up to 8 and 4 strands up to 6 (151,559 braids, no caps), in exact arithmetic, against a determinant computed independently from the reduced Burau representation.

Every prediction holds for every braid. Each bracket value is one integer times one 8th root of unity; the integer is the determinant; the bracket is zero exactly when the determinant is; and the phase obeys bracket = ζ8−3w·u·det with u = ±1 for an odd number of components and ±i for an even number. The writhe exponent comes out as −3w, not the textbook +3w, which is the mirrored crossing convention of note 2 showing up where it should; the textbook convention fails on 17,618 braids, and |Δ(+1)| in place of |Δ(−1)| fails on about half, so the tests can fail. So the DKC weight set is a determinant and one of eight phases, an eight-pointed star of integer multiples rather than a lattice, and "δ = 0 detects entanglement" is "split links have determinant zero". Enumerated completely, demo 29's catalog definition has 100 distinct values over 30 determinants, not the 64 its capped catalog found.

Future experiments

Proposed by this reading, roughly in order of cost. None changes an existing demo; each would be a new one.

  1. The Rosetta test: is the δ = 0 bracket the determinant times a writhe phase? All 64 distinct bracket values of demo 29 are a single integer times a single eighth root of unity (checked: 64 of 64), and demo 35's magnitudes are knot determinants. The classical relation ⟨L⟩ = (−A3)wVL(t), with t = A−4 = −1 here and |VL(−1)| = det(L), predicts exactly that: magnitude = determinant, phase = writhe phase times a fourth root of unity that is real for an odd number of components and imaginary for an even number. Test it on every braid of the catalogs against a determinant computed independently (Burau). If it holds, the DKC weight set is "a determinant and one of eight phases", and much of the later structure may be read from that. Done: demo 113, confirmed for all 151,559 braids.

  2. A null model for the parity result (demo 50). If parity at six sectors survives (a), the result is about eight directions and small integers, a more general statement; if it fails there, knots matter, and the claim is earned.

  3. Linear separability as a baseline. Any ranking of function classes (demos 45, 48 and onward) should be reported beside the ranking that separability alone predicts (note 17).

Looking back over these demos: the polynomial and matrix machinery of demos 1 to 9 is what makes "exact" possible later; the numerical detour of demos 10 to 25, whatever its labels, is where the idea of a bracket value as a weight first appears, and where δ = 0 was found three times over (as a zero pattern, as the gate angle, as an operating band); and demos 26 to 29 are where the idea is tested directly. The arithmetic was right everywhere it was checked. What went wrong was the labels, the interpretation, and the checks written after the results were known.
