Brighton

Visiones electae ex brighton-visio.md (Fran, 2026-09-23)

Brighton is a system for shared scholarship over texts. Fran calls it an evolution of the internet: what you might build for scholarship if you could design the web again with thirty years of hindsight.B1 Q1 It begins with Catholic texts and is meant to grow into every scholarly domain,B3 Q2 and along the way it should prove much of the "private internet" technology that rhubarb is building, the house's own web-server libraries among them.B2.1 B2.2 The work already begun on Lapide shows what one of those intentions looks like in practice: every piece of text can be traced back to the ink it came from, and every change along the way is recorded.Q1 Q12 Q14

Under every translated sentence runs a chain. The English rests on a normalized Latin sentence, which rests on the Latin as the page prints it, which rests on the words an OCR engine read and their boxes on the scan; select a sentence in one and it lights up in the others.Q4 The handle for all of it is an address for every sentence. A reference such as c1.s4.pxyz7.abcd8.2 names a chapter, a section, a paragraph and a sentence. The sentence id is a hash of the Latin as printed, so the same address finds the same sentence in English, French or Polish, and rewording a translation never changes it; when a correction changes the Latin, the old address leads to the new one, and no address ever stops resolving.B4 Q3 Translations, corrections and notes anchor to addresses, never to character offsets.Q4

So issues are filed against the structure of the text itself, and every passage carries a visible history.B8.1 Q13 Beside each translation stands the rubric it was made under: the conventions and glossary that bound the translator (how to render dulia and hyperdulia, for instance) as they stood when that sentence was translated, and the model that did the work.B8.3 Q5 Q17 Encyclopedia entries for saints, places, events and works, written by AI or by people, gather what the texts say about them.B12.1

A book lives in a single file: its text, the metadata of every sentence and its page images together, in sqlite to begin with.B9.2 Q6 There is no centre to the network. It is federated: servers that others run hold their own texts, and links reach across them.B5 Q7 A standalone desktop application lets anyone run a translation project of their own.B14 Around the texts gather their readers, in something like a social network for scholastic reading that avoids the pitfalls of social networks,B7.5 Q8 and tools for learning the languages the texts are written in.B13 Q9

Beyond texts, the same structure is meant to hold mathematics, medicine and the sciences, built from published books and papers,B15 and there the base computational artifact of any domain is a single compilable C89 file.B15.2 Q10 At the far end stands a claim graph, in which claims are linked to the claims they rest on: a disagreement shows the point where it departs, and a claim verified, disproven or qualified shows how far its effects ripple.B16 Q11

It starts with Lapide, whose translation is complete but is a first draft: every chapter reviewed so far has turned up issues, often significant ones.B17 The first version taught the hard lesson. Its model repaired errors silently, fixing them in the English while the Latin stayed wrong, and left no record of either. The rebuild takes the opposite stance. Agents do the work and people are optional, because no person will ever read every volume, so nothing may depend on a human check. In return every change is attributed to the exact model that made it, corrections against the scan are never mixed with spelling policy or with interpretation, and anything one model did can be found and redone.Q12 Q13 Q14

Quaestiones

Questions that came up while writing the prose above: mostly "why", where the source states what but not why, plus the places where the prose leans on my reading. Answers will go back into the paragraphs. On 2026-10-02 the design and process docs of ../lapide-v2 were read for answers; where they gave one it is written under the question, with its source. Those docs record decisions as Fran's, but they were made for Lapide: whether each answer holds for all of Brighton is itself worth a look.

  1. Q1B1 What would be done "more intentionally" than the web as it is: addressing, federation, the issue history, something else? Which one most?
    apertum Not answered in lapide-v2. A lead only: its design goal, "every piece of text on the site can be traced back to the ink it came from, and every change along the way is recorded" (strata-textus.md §Goal), is the kind of intention the web never had.
  2. Q2B3 Why Catholic texts first, beyond your own interest? Is there something structural about them, like dense citation of Scripture and the Fathers, that makes them the hardest or the best first test?
    apertum Not answered. Indirect evidence for the "hardest test" reading: the translation package carries every cited Scripture chapter (Douay-Rheims beside the Vulgate), the work's own sources on a local shelf (Augustine for Bede), and a glossary of theological terms (translating.md, "The package").
  3. Q3B4 Why hashes of the text rather than plain sequence numbers (c1.s4.p7.3)? A hash means ids change on every edit and need redirects. What does content identity buy that is worth that? (My guess: a mirror can compute and verify ids on its own, without a central authority.)
    responsum ex lapide-v2 Identity comes from the source, so it is the same in every language. The hash is taken over the diplomatic Latin (what the page prints): switching ~en to ~fr keeps your place, even where one language renders a sentence as two. Translations are never hashed, because they are revised far more often than the Latin is corrected; rewording one never changes an address. Hashing the diplomatic rather than the normalized text means a change of spelling policy does not re-mint thousands of addresses, and only a real correction against the scan does. Old addresses never 404: every retirement records its successors. (strata-textus.md §3.0, §3.5, §3.6, §3.4; decided by Fran 2026-09-26.)
    My guess was wrong. An address is not a pure function of the text: the .N is minting order, kept in a per-chapter mint ledger, so a mirror cannot compute ids on its own; it needs the ledger (§3.2). See Q15.
  4. Q4"Much of the rest hangs from this" is my reading, not your text. Is the address really the foundation, or is it one piece among equals?
    responsum ex lapide-v2 Yes, with one layer beneath it. "Annotations anchor to addresses, never character offsets", a lesson from v1, whose offset-anchored index broke on every edit. Translations, corrections, checks, findings, requests and crops are all keyed by address. But the address is minted from the diplomatic layer, which sits on a stack: page image → OCR words and boxes → diplomatic → normalized → translation. The deepest foundation is traceability to the ink; the address is its handle. (strata-textus.md §Goal, §1, §3.)
  5. Q5B8.3 Why should a reader see the rubric? Is it about trust in AI translation, accountability, teaching how a translation is made?
    responsum ex lapide-v2 (pro recensoribus) Accountability, and redoability. In lapide-v2 the rubric is the conventions and the glossary. They bind translators and reviewers alike ("Judge what is written, not what you would have written"); a glossary term rendered differently needs a note saying why. Everything is in the event log, "so @seq shows the checklist, glossary and policy that were in force at any point, not just the text"; the exact model behind every check is recorded, so everything one model did can be redone or distrusted by policy. (translating.md; slice 2 design, "Principles".) Lapide-v2 shows the rubric to reviewers; showing it to readers is Brighton's step. See Q17.
  6. Q6B9.2 Why one file per book? Portability, archiving (one file outlives a folder), mirroring, or something else?
    partim Lapide-v2 keeps one append-only event log per work (textus/eventa.jsonl): "a work is self-contained", the current text files are projections rebuilt from the log, and git is only transport and backup (strata-textus.md §3.7). That is the same shape as rhubarb's own ledger (truth in tabularium.jsonl, the .db a rebuildable projection). See Q16.
  7. Q7B5 Why federated: hosting cost, independence from any one owner, surviving over a long time? Is it closer to email, to Usenet, or to something older, like copies of manuscripts held in many monasteries?
  8. Q8B7.5 Which pitfalls of social networks, specifically? What should the reading community never become?
  9. Q9B13 You called language learning "not strictly necessary" but kept it. Why does it belong inside Brighton rather than beside it?
  10. Q10B15.2 Why a C89 file as the computational artifact of every domain, where others would use notebook languages? Longevity, auditability, one toolchain for everything?
  11. Q11B16 Does the claim graph belong inside Brighton, or is it a separate project (the proof checker) that Brighton links into?
  12. Q12B17 "That is the work Brighton has to make tractable first" is my inference. Is trustworthy AI translation the central problem Brighton solves, with the rubrics, the issue history and the page images all serving it? If so, it should lead the first paragraph.
    responsum ex lapide-v2 For Lapide, clearly yes. The first principle of the review design: "Agents do the work; people are optional. Lapide v1 was translated in 2–3 months. No person reads every volume, so nothing here may require a human check." The failure it answers is v1's silent repair: the model "fixed errors in the English (transcunt → 'pass on') and left the Latin wrong, with no record". Hence the three kinds of change that never mix (evidence-based corrections, normalization policy, interpretation), every change attributed to its exact model, and old addresses that always resolve. (Slice 2 design, "Principles"; strata-textus.md §Goal, §1; surya-2.md.) Whether it is the centre of all of Brighton, or of its publishing side only, is Q13 and Q14.

Quaestiones novae (from reading lapide-v2)

  1. Q13B8.1 Q12 Lapide-v2 is built so that no human check is ever required. Brighton opens issues to the public. Are readers the human check the pipeline deliberately does not depend on: optional, but welcome, and recorded when they happen?
  2. Q14Q12 "The three kinds of change never mix" (evidence, policy, interpretation): is that a principle of all of Brighton, holding for the encyclopedia and the claim graph too, or a rule of the translation pipeline only?
  3. Q15B5 Q3 An address needs the per-chapter mint ledger to resolve. Does that make federation the replication of event logs: a mirror holds a work's log (and its images), not just its text?
  4. Q16B9.2 Q6 Is the .brighton file the event log plus the page images, with sqlite as a projection rebuilt from it (as tabularium does with its journal), or is the sqlite file itself the truth?
  5. Q17B8.3 Q5 What would a reader see of the rubric: the conventions and glossary in force when that sentence was translated, read at its @seq? Does a reader need the rubric to trust a sentence, or is it there for the reader who wants to dig?
